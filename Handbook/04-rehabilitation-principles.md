# Rehabilitation Principles

> "Rehabilitation is not about surviving today's workout. It is about building a shoulder that remains stable for the next twenty years."

---

# Purpose

Every exercise in ReForge follows a small number of rehabilitation principles.

Understanding these principles helps you make better decisions during training and reduces the risk of setbacks.

Never judge progress by how tired you become.

Judge progress by how stable, controlled, and confident your shoulder feels.

---

# Principle 1 — Stability Before Strength

A powerful muscle cannot compensate for an unstable joint.

Before lifting heavier weights, your shoulder must first learn to keep the humeral head centered inside the socket.

This is why the first phase emphasizes:

- Rotator cuff activation
- Scapular control
- Slow movement
- Isometric holds
- Light resistance

Strength is built on stability.

---

# Principle 2 — Quality Over Quantity

Ten perfect repetitions are more valuable than thirty poor repetitions.

Every repetition should look nearly identical.

If technique begins to deteriorate:

- Reduce resistance
- Reduce repetitions
- Slow down

Perfect movement is always the goal.

---

# Principle 3 — Pain Is Information

Pain is not something to ignore.

It is feedback.

Use this simple guide.

### 0–2 / 10

Normal.

Continue the exercise.

---

### 3–4 / 10

Pay attention.

Reduce resistance or range of motion.

Monitor symptoms.

---

### 5 / 10 or higher

Stop.

Replace the exercise with an easier variation or end the session.

Never push through significant shoulder pain.

---

# Principle 4 — Protect the Front of the Shoulder

Your history of repeated anterior dislocations means protecting the front of the shoulder is always the highest priority.

Avoid forcing the shoulder into vulnerable positions.

Examples include:

- Deep bench presses
- Wide dips
- Heavy overhead pressing
- Explosive throwing
- Fast uncontrolled movements

Control comes before range.

---

# Principle 5 — Scapula First

The shoulder blade is the foundation of the shoulder.

If the scapula moves poorly, the shoulder joint becomes unstable.

Every workout should include exercises that improve:

- Scapular upward rotation
- Retraction
- Posterior tilt
- Serratus anterior activation
- Lower trapezius activation

Strong shoulder blades create stable shoulders.

---

# Principle 6 — Slow Movement Wins

Use controlled tempo.

Default tempo:

2 seconds lifting

3 seconds lowering

Pause briefly when required.

Avoid momentum.

The muscles—not speed—should move the weight.

---

# Principle 7 — Respect Fatigue

Fatigue reduces coordination.

Reduced coordination increases instability.

If your shoulder begins feeling:

- Loose
- Heavy
- Unstable
- Difficult to control

Stop the exercise.

Quality decreases before injury occurs.

Learn to recognize that warning.

---

# Principle 8 — Progress Slowly

The shoulder heals more slowly than motivation.

Only increase difficulty when:

- Pain remains low
- Technique is consistent
- Stability has improved
- Current workload feels easy

Progression should feel almost boring.

That is usually the safest speed.

---

# Principle 9 — Posture Supports Recovery

Good posture does not mean standing perfectly straight.

It means allowing the shoulder blade to move freely.

During the day:

- Keep the chest relaxed
- Let the shoulders rest naturally
- Avoid prolonged rounded posture
- Change positions frequently

Movement is healthier than perfect posture.

---

# Principle 10 — Recovery Is Part of Training

Muscles adapt between workouts.

Recovery includes:

- Sleep
- Nutrition
- Hydration
- Walking
- Stress management

Missing recovery is the same as missing training.

---

# Principle 11 — Consistency Beats Intensity

Small improvements repeated every week produce enormous changes over time.

One perfect workout changes very little.

One hundred consistent workouts change everything.

The goal is never perfection.

The goal is returning tomorrow.

---

# The ReForge Rule

Whenever you are uncertain during an exercise, ask yourself:

> "Does this movement make my shoulder feel more stable or less stable?"

If the answer is **more stable**, continue.

If the answer is **less stable**, modify the exercise or stop.

Your shoulder should finish each workout feeling more controlled than when you started—not more vulnerable.
